How the numbers are worked out

This is textbook psychrometry, the physics of air and water vapor. Every formula and constant below is a published standard. None are ours.

  1. Saturation vapor pressure. The most water vapor air can hold depends only on temperature. The page takes it from the saturation equation of IAPWS-IF97, the international steam-table standard. It is valid from 0 °C (32 °F) up, which covers every sauna.
  2. Vapor pressure now. Relative humidity is the share of that maximum which is in use. Vapor pressure = saturation pressure × relative humidity.
  3. Dew point. The temperature at which that vapor pressure would be saturation. Any surface cooler than the dew point collects condensation. The page gets it from the inverse steam-table equation, so no approximation is added.
  4. Absolute humidity. Grams of water vapor in a cubic meter of air, from the ideal gas law with the gas constant of water vapor, 461.5 J/(kg·K).
  5. Water to raise the humidity. Room volume × the rise in absolute humidity. One gram of water is one milliliter.
  6. The ceiling above the boiling point. Vapor pressure cannot be higher than the pressure in the room, and a sauna is open to the outside air. At standard sea-level pressure, relative humidity can reach 100% only up to 212 °F (100 °C). Above that the most it can be is air pressure divided by saturation pressure: about 71% at 230 °F (110 °C). The page will not accept a reading above that limit.

The Magnus formula is what most weather and dew point calculators use. Its usual coefficients were fitted for −40 to 50 °C, so it reads high in a hot sauna. That is why this page uses the steam-table equation instead.

The ideal gas law is also an approximation. Against the same NIST tables it reads about 0.9% low for saturated vapor at 80 °C and 1.6% low at 100 °C, and less than that at ordinary sauna humidity. Either error is small next to what vents and wood do in a real room.

What this page does not know. How fast your room changes its air, how dry the wood is, how hot the stones are, where your hygrometer hangs and how accurate it is. Sauna air is not evenly mixed: it is hotter and wetter near the ceiling right after a pour. The page assumes standard sea-level air pressure; at altitude the ceiling on humidity above the boiling point is lower. It does not give a "feels like" temperature. There is no agreed formula for one at sauna conditions, so we do not print one.
